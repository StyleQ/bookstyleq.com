Most administrators don't think about their salon vendor until something forces the issue: a family complaint, a resident who stops asking for appointments, or a survey question that catches them off guard. By then, the problem has usually been building for months.

A salon program is a small line item compared to staffing or food service, but it touches residents directly and often, which makes it a disproportionate source of word-of-mouth risk. Here are five signs your current vendor has quietly become a liability, not an amenity.

## 1. Appointments feel rushed

This is the complaint we hear most often from families: a resident goes in for a haircut and comes out looking nothing like themselves. It's usually not a skill problem. It's a pacing problem. Corporate vendors frequently book stylists back-to-back to maximize visits per day, which leaves no room to do the job well for someone who needs extra time, patience, or a gentler touch.

## 2. Your staff has become the go-between

If your nursing staff is tracking down residents for appointments, rescheduling missed visits, or fielding complaints on the vendor's behalf, the vendor has shifted its coordination work onto your building. That's a cost, even if it never shows up on an invoice. A salon program should require zero lift from your team: no scheduling, no reminders, no playing messenger.

![A stylist carefully combing a resident's hair in a calm, unhurried moment.](/images/svc-hair-6.jpg)
*The difference between a rushed visit and a good one is rarely skill. It's time.*

## 3. Inconsistency is becoming a compliance conversation

A different stylist every visit, inconsistent infection-control practices, or no documentation to point to if a surveyor asks: these are the details that turn a beauty amenity into a regulatory question. You shouldn't have to chase down proof that a vendor is licensed, insured, and trained for elder care. That should be handled before the question is ever asked.

> The real question isn't whether residents love their hair. It's whether you can explain, calmly and with documentation, why a vendor's service is consistent, every time, to anyone who asks.

## 4. Family complaints are reaching your office directly

Word of mouth in senior living is ruthless. One bad experience doesn't stay contained to one resident; it travels through the family group chat and the dining room before you hear about it. If complaints about the salon are landing on your desk instead of being resolved quietly by the vendor, the relationship has already broken down.

## 5. You can't remember the last time you heard from the vendor at all

A vendor that only shows up to provide the service, with no check-ins, no feedback loop, and no visibility into how residents are actually experiencing it, is a vendor you're flying blind with. You find out something's wrong when a family does, not before.

![A resident receiving a gentle, attentive haircut from a stylist.](/images/svc-hair-7.jpg)

## What a better vendor relationship looks like

None of this means your residents need a fancier salon. It means the vendor behind it needs to be accountable in ways that are easy to verify and don't require your staff to do extra work:

- Licensed, insured, and background-checked, with documentation you can produce on request.
- Stylists trained for elder care: memory care, limited mobility, and sensitive skin.
- Scheduling, reminders, and resident coordination handled entirely by the vendor.
- The same familiar stylist for each resident, with visits paced so no one feels rushed.
- Billing sent directly to residents or families, never to your facility's ledger.
- A feedback loop that surfaces a problem to the vendor before it reaches a family.

If you're reading this near a contract renewal or notice window, you don't need to overhaul anything to find out whether a better option exists. A short pilot in a single unit, assisted living or memory care, is usually enough to tell you more than any sales pitch could.
